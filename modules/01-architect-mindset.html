In every batch I teach, someone asks on the first morning: "Sir, which services do I need to memorise for AZ-305?" My honest answer: fewer than you think. The exam and the job both reward a way of thinking far more than a list of service names.

Mindset takeaway

01.1

Same problem, three lenses

Let me give you a situation I've seen at more than one company. The business head walks in and says:

"Our online store becomes painfully slow every Friday evening. Fix it."A very real Monday-morning email

Now watch how three smart people react to the same sentence.

LensFirst instinctWhat they might miss
Developer"Let me profile the code. Probably a slow SQL query or a missing index."It might not be the code at all. And fixing one query doesn't stop the next Friday surprise.
Operations"Add more VMs. Bigger SKU on the database."Cost doubles for a problem that lasts three hours a week. Scaling the wrong layer changes nothing.
Solution architect"How slow, for whom, and what does it cost us? Is it a predictable peak? What does 'good' look like, in numbers?"Nothing yet, because the architect hasn't decided anything. They're still collecting the facts the decision depends on.

The architect will probably end up with a mix: autoscale rules tied to the Friday pattern, a cache in front of the product catalogue, a CDN for images, an alert when page response crosses a threshold, and a monthly review of the cost. But they got there by asking questions, not by guessing a fix.

Developer view

The system is code. Problems are bugs. Success is "it works on my machine and passes tests".

Architect view

The system is a set of decisions serving a business. Problems are risks to an outcome. Success is "it meets the agreed targets at an acceptable cost, and we can prove it".

Not a ranking

None of these lenses is "better". A good architect has usually worn the other two hats. The point is that AZ-305 tests the third one, and you have to consciously switch to it, especially if you come from a hands-on admin or developer background.

01.2

What a solution architect actually does

Definition

A solution architect translates business requirements into a technical design that is secure, reliable, cost-effective and operable, and is accountable for the trade-offs made along the way.

In plain words

You're the person who decides what gets built and why it's shaped that way, so that the people who build and run it don't have to guess. You don't need to write the code yourself. You do need to know when the plan is going to hurt someone later.

Imagine this

You're building a house. The mason knows bricks. The electrician knows wiring. The interior designer knows tiles and colours. The architect is the one who first asks: How many people will live here? Do you plan to have kids? Is this an earthquake zone? What's the budget, really? Will you add a floor in five years?

Only after that do they draw the plan. And if the family says "we want a swimming pool on the third floor", the architect is the one who explains, calmly, what that does to the foundation and the budget.

The four things you're paid for

ClarityTurning vague wishes ("it should be fast") into measurable targets ("95% of pages load in under 2 seconds").
OptionsAlways having at least two viable designs, so the choice is a choice and not an accident.
Trade-offsKnowing what each option costs: in money, risk, complexity and future flexibility.
AccountabilityWriting the decision down, with reasons, so it can be defended or revisited later.
01.3

Requirements: the iceberg

Definition

Functional requirements describe what a system must do. Non-functional requirements (NFRs, also called quality attributes) describe how well it must do it: availability, performance, security, compliance, cost, recoverability and so on.

Business people talk almost entirely in functional requirements: "customers should be able to search, pay and track orders". Those are the tip of the iceberg. The bulk of an architect's work, and nearly all of the AZ-305 exam, lives below the waterline.

Questions that pull requirements above water

  • Availability: "If this is down for an hour on a Tuesday, what does that cost you? On Black Friday?"
  • Data loss: "If we lose the last 15 minutes of orders, is that an inconvenience or a disaster?"
  • Compliance: "Which regulations apply? Where are your customers? Where may the data physically live?"
  • Security: "Who must never see this data, including our own admins?"
  • Scale: "What does peak look like today, and in three years?"
  • Operations: "Who runs this at 2 a.m.? What skills does that team have today?"
  • Cost: "Is there a budget ceiling, or a preference for predictable monthly spend over lowest possible spend?"
Exam cue

AZ-305 case studies hide NFRs inside long paragraphs: "The company plans to expand into Germany" (data residency), "auditors must be able to review access quarterly" (access reviews), "developers must not store credentials in code" (managed identities and Key Vault). Train yourself to underline the NFRs as you read.

01.4

The Well-Architected Framework

Definition

The Azure Well-Architected Framework (WAF) is a set of guiding tenets for building high-quality workloads, organised into five pillars: Reliability, Security, Cost Optimization, Operational Excellence and Performance Efficiency.

In plain words

It's a checklist of the five things that go wrong with cloud systems. If you've thought about all five on purpose, your design is defensible. If you've ignored one, that's where the trouble will come from.

The pillars in one line each

PillarThe question it asksTypical Azure levers
ReliabilityWhat happens when a component, zone or region fails?Availability zones, multi-region, backups, Site Recovery, health probes, retries
SecurityWho can reach what, and how do we know?Entra ID, Conditional Access, RBAC, Key Vault, private endpoints, Defender for Cloud
Cost OptimizationAre we paying for value or for waste?Right-sizing, reservations and savings plans, autoscale, budgets, tags, Azure Advisor
Operational ExcellenceCan we change and run it safely and repeatably?Infrastructure as Code, CI/CD, Azure Policy, Azure Monitor, runbooks
Performance EfficiencyDoes it meet response targets as load changes?Autoscale, caching, CDN, right data store, load testing

Pillars pull against each other

This is the part people skip, and it's the most important. The pillars are not independent. Improving one almost always costs you something in another.

Reliability ↔ Cost

A second region doubles a big chunk of your bill. Worth it for payments; probably not for the internal canteen-menu app.

Security ↔ Operational ease

Private endpoints everywhere and just-in-time admin access are safer, but every deployment and every 2 a.m. fix now has extra steps.

Performance ↔ Cost

Premium SKUs and over-provisioning make things fast and expensive. Caching can make things fast and cheaper, at the cost of complexity.

Reliability ↔ Performance

Synchronous replication across regions protects data but adds latency to every write. Asynchronous is faster but can lose recent data.

Mindset takeaway

"Best practice" without context is a myth. The right design is the one whose trade-offs the business has knowingly accepted.

  2. On Overview, point at the Advisor score and the category tiles: Reliability Security Performance Cost Operational excellence. These are the five pillars, almost word for word.
  3. Open the category with the most recommendations, open one recommendation, and show its impact, the affected resources and the suggested action.

01.5

SLA, SLO, RTO, RPO: putting numbers on "reliable"

"Highly available" means nothing until it has a number. These four terms appear in almost every module from here on, so let's nail them down now.

SLA (Service Level Agreement)A provider's contractual commitment, e.g. "99.99% uptime per month, or we refund service credits". Microsoft publishes one per service.
SLO (Service Level Objective)Your internal target for your own workload, e.g. "99.9% of checkout requests succeed". Usually set tighter than your SLA to customers.
SLI (Service Level Indicator)The actual measurement: what percentage of requests really did succeed last month.
RTO (Recovery Time Objective)The maximum acceptable time the workload can be down after a disaster.
RPO (Recovery Point Objective)The maximum acceptable amount of data loss, measured in time. "RPO 15 min" = we can lose at most the last 15 minutes of data.
Imagine this

You're writing a long document and the laptop dies. RPO is how much you lose: if auto-save runs every 10 minutes, you lose up to 10 minutes of typing. RTO is how long until you're typing again: borrow a colleague's laptop (fast) or wait for the repair shop (slow). Two completely different problems, solved with two different investments.

What the nines really mean

People throw around "four nines" casually. Here's what each one actually allows. Keep this table handy; it calms down a lot of meeting-room arguments.

AvailabilityDowntime per monthDowntime per yearFeels like
99%~7.2 hours~3.65 daysFine for internal tools
99.9%~43 minutes~8.8 hoursTypical single-instance PaaS
99.95%~22 minutes~4.4 hoursA well-built single-region app
99.99%~4.3 minutes~53 minutesZones or multiple regions, serious money
99.999%~26 seconds~5.3 minutesMulti-region active-active, rare and expensive
01.6

Composite SLA: the maths every architect should do once

Definition

A composite SLA is the expected availability of a workload that depends on several services. For services in a chain (all must work), multiply their availabilities. For redundant copies (any one is enough), availability = 1 − (probability that all copies fail).

Common trap

A junior design proudly lists "App Service 99.95%" as the app's availability, forgetting the database, the storage account, the Key Vault and the identity provider it also depends on. Your workload is never more available than the product of everything it can't live without.

Exam cue

SLA questions are quick wins if you remember two rules: multiply for dependencies, 1 − (failure × failure) for redundancy. Also remember that most single-instance SLAs only apply when you follow the documented configuration, e.g. VMs need premium SSD or two or more instances across zones or an availability set to get their higher SLA.

Portal demoPrice one design two ways, then find its SLA~5 min

Goal: Make the reliability-versus-cost trade-off visible in rupees, not just percentages.

  1. Open azure.microsoft.com/pricing/calculator in a new tab.
  2. Add App Service: Premium v3, P1v3, 2 instances, Central India. Add Azure SQL Database: single database, General Purpose, 2 vCores, same region. Note the monthly total.
  3. Now build the two-region version: add the same two services again in South India, plus Azure Front Door as the global entry point. Compare the new total.
  4. Search the web for “Service Level Agreements for Online Services” (Microsoft licensing site) and look up the SLA for App Service and SQL Database.
  5. On the whiteboard: multiply the two for one region, then use 1 − (failure × failure) for two regions, and put the price difference next to the extra nines.

Point out while you are there

  • Roughly double the cost for one or two extra nines. That is the conversation you must have with the business, with numbers.
  • SLAs depend on configuration (tier, zone redundancy), which is why the calculator and the SLA page go together.

Say: “The price of one more nine is right there on the screen. Now ask the business whether an hour of outage costs more than that.”

01.7

Trade-offs and decisions

"It depends" is a famous architect joke. Here's the part people forget: "it depends" is where your answer starts, not where it ends. The professional version is "it depends on X and Y, and given what you've told me, I recommend Z because…".

One-way doors and two-way doors

Two-way door (reversible)

VM size, App Service plan tier, autoscale thresholds, alert rules. Easy to change later. Decide quickly, measure, adjust.

One-way door (hard to reverse)

Tenant design, management group hierarchy, primary region, IP address plan, database engine, identity model. Slow down, involve stakeholders, write it down.

Notice how many of the one-way doors are exactly what Part I of this handbook covers: governance, identity and the foundation. That's not an accident. Get these wrong and everything built on top inherits the mistake.

Write it down: the decision record

Good architects leave a trail. A lightweight Architecture Decision Record (ADR) is enough. Months later, when someone asks "why on earth did we pick this?", the answer is there, with the context that made it sensible at the time.

A one-page ADR template
  1. Context: what problem, which requirements and constraints.
  2. Options considered: at least two, with pros and cons.
  3. Decision: what we chose.
  4. Consequences: what we gain, what we accept, what we must now watch.
  5. Revisit when: the trigger that should reopen this decision (cost doubles, new region launches, regulation changes).
Developer view

"I'll use Cosmos DB because it's fast and I like it."

Architect view

"Cosmos DB, because we need multi-region writes with single-digit-millisecond latency for global users. We accept higher cost and a team learning curve. Revisit if we stay in one region for 12 months."

The architect's toolkit

You don't have to invent everything from scratch. Microsoft gives you a set of free tools that experienced architects use all the time.

Well-Architected ReviewA free self-assessment questionnaire that scores a workload against the five pillars.
Azure AdvisorPersonalised recommendations from your actual environment, grouped by the same five pillars.
Pricing Calculator & TCO CalculatorEstimate run cost before you build, and compare against on-premises total cost of ownership.

Imagine this

CAF is the town planning department: zoning rules, roads, water and electricity for the whole city. WAF is the building code you check each individual house against. A city needs both, and so does a cloud estate.

01.9

Reading an AZ-305 question like an architect

AZ-305 questions are rarely about "what does service X do?". They're about "which of these four valid services is best here?". The difference is always hidden in a few words.

  1. Read the last line first. What is being asked: a service, a configuration, a sequence? Now you know what to look for.
  2. Underline the requirement words. "Must", "minimise", "ensure", "without".
  3. Circle the constraint words. Cost, admin effort, code changes, existing licences, region.
  4. Eliminate the over-engineered and the under-powered. Two options usually fall away immediately.
  5. Pick the option that meets every "must" with the least of whatever is being minimised.

The keyword decoder

When the question says…It's usually pushing you towards…
"Minimise administrative effort"PaaS or managed services over IaaS; built-in features over custom scripts.
"Minimise cost"The cheapest option that still meets every requirement, never the cheapest overall.
"Without modifying the application code"Lift-and-shift, platform-level features, configuration rather than refactoring.
"Must remain available if a region fails"Multi-region deployment plus geo-replicated data and a global entry point.
"Must remain available if a datacentre fails"Availability zones (zone-redundant services) in one region.
"Ensure compliance" / "prevent"Azure Policy with a deny-type effect, not a report after the fact.
"Least privilege"The narrowest built-in role at the narrowest scope, ideally time-bound (PIM).
"No credentials stored in code"Managed identity, with Key Vault for anything that genuinely must be a secret.
Try one

"A company runs a stateless web app in one Azure region. It must stay available if a single datacentre fails. The solution must minimise cost. What should you recommend?"

Underline: datacentre (not region) and minimise cost. A second region would work but is over-engineered and pricier. The answer is to deploy across availability zones in the existing region.

Portal demoWhere availability zones appear when you create things~2 min

Goal: Connect the exam phrase “if a datacentre fails” to a real setting on the first tab of a create form.

  1. Search Virtual machines › + Create › Azure virtual machine.
  2. On Basics, pick a region with zones (for example Central India) and open Availability options: no redundancy, availability zone, scale set, availability set.
  3. Choose Availability zone and show that you can select zone 1, 2 or 3 (picking several creates one VM per zone).
  4. Switch the region to one without zones and show that the zone option disappears. Then cancel; don't create anything.

Point out while you are there

  • Zone choice sits on the very first tab: reliability is a day-zero decision, not a later fix.
  • PaaS services show this as a zone redundancy switch instead (for example App Service Premium plans and Azure SQL).

Say: “When the exam says ‘a datacentre fails’, this dropdown is the answer. When it says ‘a region fails’, you need a second region.”

01.10

Meet Meridian Retail, our case company

Abstract knowledge fades. Decisions about a real company stick. So from here on, every module ends by applying what we learned to one fictional but very realistic organisation.

Meridian Retail· the case we'll design for throughout this handbook

A 30-year-old retail chain with 180 stores across India, headquartered in Bengaluru, now launching a serious online store and opening a subsidiary in Germany.

  • Three business units: Stores, Online and Logistics, each with its own IT budget.
  • Today: an on-premises datacentre with ~600 VMs, Active Directory, SQL Server, file shares, and a lot of "temporary" scripts from 2014.
  • Leadership wants: move to Azure within 18 months; predictable monthly cost per business unit; online checkout that survives a regional outage; pass a PCI DSS audit for card payments; keep EU customer data in the EU.
  • Pain points: nobody knows who owns half the servers, admins share accounts, and outages are discovered by customers on social media.

Read that list again with the iceberg in mind. Almost everything leadership asked for is non-functional. And the pain points map neatly onto the next three modules:

Pain pointModule that fixes it
"Nobody knows who owns what", "cost per business unit"02 · Governance
"Admins share accounts", "PCI audit", "EU partners need access"03 · Identity & Access
"Customers find outages before we do"04 · Monitoring & Logging
01.11

Recap and self-check

Architect's checklist for any new design

  • I can state the business outcome in one sentence.
  • I've listed the non-functional requirements, with numbers where possible.
  • I know the constraints: budget, compliance, skills, timeline.
  • I've compared at least two options against all five WAF pillars.
  • I've calculated a rough composite SLA and checked it against the target.
  • I know which of my decisions are one-way doors, and I've documented those.

Self-check

1. A stakeholder says "the system must be highly available". What do you do next?

Turn it into a number and a context: "What availability percentage? Over what period? Measured how? What does an hour of downtime cost?" Only then can you choose between a single zone, availability zones and multiple regions.

2. Your app (99.95%) depends on a database (99.99%) and a storage account (99.9%). Rough composite SLA?

0.9995 × 0.9999 × 0.999 ≈ 99.84%. The weakest dependency drags everything down, so that's where to invest first.

3. The business can tolerate 4 hours of downtime but only 5 minutes of data loss. Which number is RTO and which is RPO?

RTO = 4 hours (time to recover). RPO = 5 minutes (data loss window). This combination calls for frequent or continuous data replication, but a cold or warm standby for compute could be acceptable.

4. Which WAF pillar is most at risk if every developer has Owner access to production "to move faster"?

Security first, with Operational Excellence a close second: untracked manual changes make the environment unpredictable. This is exactly the trade-off governance and identity (Modules 02 and 03) help you manage.

5. Name one one-way-door decision Meridian Retail must get right early.

Any of: the Entra tenant model, the management group hierarchy, the subscription strategy per business unit, the primary Azure region, or the IP address plan for hybrid connectivity.

Carry this into Module 02

Before anyone deploys anything for Meridian, we need to decide how the cloud is organised: who owns what, who pays for what, and which rules can't be broken. That's governance, and that's next.
