The Architect's Casebook: Real Briefs, Your Design

Twelve modules taught you the services. This is where you use them. Each case is a real-feeling business brief: the company, the people, the numbers, the mess they're in today. You read it the way an architect would, pick out what actually constrains the design, and make the decisions in Azure. Then you submit, and the casebook shows you the reference architecture, why every option was right or wrong, and how the same design looks on AWS and Google Cloud.

How it works

Read, decide, submit, compare

  1. Read the case, in either view. Case brief gives the story, voices from the business, key numbers and a diagram of how things work today. Discovery session follows the same case as a client workshop: Raushan, the Azure Solution Architect, listens, asks clarifying questions, writes down requirements and constraints, then recommends services and records each decision.
  2. Step 1: spot the constraints. Tick the phrases that really limit the design. Background details are mixed in on purpose.
  3. Step 2: make the decisions. One answer per decision, in Azure. Notice which words push you to an answer.
  4. Submit. You get your score, the right answer for each decision, an explanation for every option, and the trigger words you should have spotted.
  5. Study the reference design. The target architecture, the configuration that matters, and what was left out on purpose.
  6. Compare clouds. One button shows the AWS and Google Cloud equivalent of each decision, and when the answer would differ.
  7. Stuck? Each decision has a Reveal hint button: a guiding question, with the key phrases highlighted in the brief. After you submit, every decision explains the service, why it fits and how to reason to it, often with a picture.
In plain words

There's no trick wording. Every right answer is something taught in the modules. The skill being tested is reading a business story and hearing the requirement inside each sentence. Your answers stay in your browser; nothing is sent anywhere.

Case 1

Tulsi Diagnostics: Reports that must never leak, change or disappear

Healthcare · identity, governance, storage, monitoring~25 minDraws on Module 02 · Module 03 · Module 04 · Module 05

The brief

Tulsi Diagnostics is a 15-year-old pathology lab chain. It runs one central lab in Pune and 70 sample collection centres across Maharashtra and Karnataka. About 1,200 staff work at collection counters and in the lab, and the company produces around 9,000 test reports a day as PDFs of about 400 KB each.

Today the lab information system (LIS) runs on two servers in the Pune lab. When a report is ready, the front office emails the PDF from a shared mailbox to the patient and, for referred patients, to the doctor. Each collection centre has one shared login on its counter PC. Last quarter a report was emailed to the wrong patient, a local newspaper picked it up, and nobody could say which staff member had sent it.

The board has approved a move to Azure with a clear brief: a secure online portal where patients see their own reports, doctors from 40 partner hospitals see reports for patients they referred, and staff work under their own names. Legal adds that reports must be kept for 10 years and must provably never change, and that all patient data must stay in India.

Usage is predictable. Reports are opened heavily in the first week and almost never afterwards. Requests for reports older than two years come in a few times a month, and the support team says a next-day turnaround for those is fine. The IT team is two people who know Windows and some .NET. They have never run Kubernetes and don't want to.

Step 1 Spot the constraints

Tick every phrase that is a real design constraint, something that limits or decides your options. Leave background details unticked.

Hint. Constraints usually carry words like must, never, only, within, at most, will not, no appetite, or a hard number. Background facts tell you about the company but wouldn't change the design if they were different. Ask of each phrase: if this were false, would I design differently?

Pick one answer for each decision. Think about which words in the brief point you there; you'll see the trigger words after you submit.

Decision 1Staff at collection counters must stop sharing logins, and the CEO wants to know who opened what. What do you design?

Hint. Look for the sentence about how staff sign in today, and what the CEO wants to know afterwards. Can a shared account ever answer 'who'?

The phrases to think about are now highlighted in the brief.

Why B is the answer

Microsoft Entra ID with Conditional Access

What it is. Entra ID is Microsoft's cloud directory: every person gets their own account that Azure and Microsoft 365 trust. Conditional Access is its policy engine. At every sign-in it checks signals (who, which app, which device, where from, how risky) and then allows, blocks or asks for MFA.

Why it fits here. The real problem is accountability. Individual accounts put a name on every action. MFA makes a stolen password useless. A Conditional Access policy can say 'staff may open the portal only from a compliant device or a known centre network'.

How to think it through

  1. The brief says one login is shared by a whole centre, so no log can name a person.
  2. Fix identity first: one account per human.
  3. Then make sign-in strong (MFA) and conditional (device, location) with Conditional Access, which needs Entra ID P1.
  4. Rule out options that keep sharing, or that use a customer identity product for employees.

Trigger words in the brief: "one shared login""who opened what""1,200 staff"

The architect's habit: Accountability starts with one identity per human. Then strengthen sign-in (MFA) and add conditions (Conditional Access).

  4. Name check: Azure AD B2C is closed to new customers.

Decision 4Where and how do you store the reports so they're kept 10 years, provably unchanged, in India, at the lowest sensible cost?

Hint. Four phrases matter: how long, 'provably', where, and how often old reports are read. Which storage feature turns 'provably never change' into something you can show an auditor?

The phrases to think about are now highlighted in the brief.

Why B is the answer

Blob Storage immutability + lifecycle management

What it is. Blob Storage is Azure's object storage for files. An immutability policy (time-based retention) makes blobs write-once, read-many (WORM): once the policy is locked, nobody can change or delete them until the period ends. A lifecycle management policy moves blobs to cheaper tiers (Cool, Cold, Archive) or deletes them based on age.

Why it fits here. The locked 10-year policy is the proof legal asked for. The lifecycle rule follows the real access pattern: Hot for the busy first month, Cool after 30 days, Archive after 2 years, when a next-day wait is acceptable. GRS keeps a second copy in South India, still inside India. Archive isn't available on ZRS or GZRS accounts, which is why GRS is chosen.

How to think it through

  1. 'Provably never change' means immutability, not backup or soft delete.
  2. 'Stay in India' limits redundancy: Central India pairs with South India, so GRS is fine.
  3. 'Almost never after a week' and 'next day is fine' allow Cool and then Archive.
  4. Check compatibility: Archive needs LRS, GRS or RA-GRS.

Trigger words in the brief: "10 years""provably never change""stay in India""almost never afterwards""next-day turnaround for those is fine"

The architect's habit: Retention and immutability are different from backup. Tiers follow the access pattern, and redundancy must respect the residency rule.

Decision 5Where does the portal run, given a two-person team with Windows and .NET skills and no Kubernetes?

Hint. Underline what the team can and can't do. Then ask: which option needs the least operating work for one web portal?

The phrases to think about are now highlighted in the brief.

Why B is the answer

Azure App Service

What it is. App Service is a platform as a service (PaaS) for web apps and APIs. You deploy code or a container; Azure runs the servers, patches the operating system and handles scaling, TLS certificates and deployment slots.

Why it fits here. A two-person Windows and .NET team gets a production-grade portal without patching servers or learning Kubernetes. Premium v3 supports zone redundancy, VNet integration to reach private endpoints, and a managed identity so the portal needs no passwords.

How to think it through

  1. The workload is one web portal.
  2. The team is small and has no Kubernetes skills, which rules out AKS.
  3. VMs would work but add patching and scaling work.
  4. PaaS with zone redundancy and a managed identity gives the most for the least effort.

Trigger words in the brief: "two people""never run Kubernetes""Windows and some .NET"

The architect's habit: Pick the least operational burden that meets the requirement. Skills are a constraint, not an afterthought.

Decision 6How do you make sure nobody, under deadline pressure, creates a storage account or database outside India?

Hint. Which sentence is a rule that must hold no matter who is deploying or how rushed they are? Rules like that shouldn't depend on people remembering.

The phrases to think about are now highlighted in the brief.

Why A is the answer

Azure Policy (Deny effect)

What it is. Azure Policy evaluates every create or update request against rules you assign. With the Deny effect, a non-compliant request is rejected, whoever sends it. Assigned at a management group, a policy applies to every subscription below it.

Why it fits here. 'Allowed locations' with only Central India and South India makes it impossible to create a resource in any other region, even by an Owner in a hurry. Because it's assigned at the management group, new subscriptions inherit it automatically.

How to think it through

  1. 'All patient data must stay in India' is a must-always-hold rule.
  2. Documents and meetings don't stop deployments; the platform must.
  3. RBAC decides who can act; Policy decides what is allowed. This is a 'what'.
  4. Assign it as high as possible so future subscriptions are covered.

Trigger words in the brief: "must stay in India"

The architect's habit: Rules that must always hold become Policy with Deny, assigned as high as possible.

Why B is the answer

Application Insights availability tests + alerts

What it is. Application Insights is Azure Monitor's application performance monitoring. An availability test calls your site from several Azure locations every few minutes, like a robot user. An alert rule watches the results, and an action group decides who is told (SMS, email, Teams, webhook).

Why it fits here. The portal is checked every 5 minutes from several places. If it fails, the on-call person gets an SMS before patients start calling. Logs stay in a Log Analytics workspace in Central India to respect the residency rule.

How to think it through

  1. The pain is 'we found out from patients'.
  2. You need a signal that represents the patient's experience: a synthetic test.
  3. A signal is useless unless it reaches a person: alert rule plus action group.
  4. Service Health and Advisor cover Azure itself or best practice, not your app.

Trigger words in the brief: "patients started calling""found out"

The architect's habit: Monitoring is designed, not installed: decide the signal, the threshold and who gets woken up.

Decision 3How do you stop the database from being flattened at 9:00:30, while fans get a fair answer instead of an error?

Why A is the answer

Service Bus queue (queue-based load levelling)

What it is. Service Bus is Azure's enterprise message broker. A queue stores messages durably until a consumer takes them. Queue-based load levelling uses a queue to absorb bursts, so the back end works at a steady, safe rate.

Why it fits here. Thousands of booking requests a second land in the queue in milliseconds, and each fan instantly hears 'you're in the queue'. Workers take requests at the rate the database can handle. If a worker restarts, nothing is lost, because a message stays until it's completed.

How to think it through

  2. Scaling the database alone can't keep up and is expensive.
  3. Put a durable buffer in between: a queue.
  4. Event Grid pushes events straight away; it doesn't hold work back to be taken at a steady pace.

Decision 4Where do seats live so that two workers can never confirm the same seat?

Why D is the answer

Azure SQL Database (transactions)

What it is. Azure SQL Database is a fully managed relational database based on the SQL Server engine. Relational databases support ACID transactions: a group of changes either all happen or none do, and two transactions can't both change the same row at once.

Why it fits here. A worker runs one statement: 'mark seat 14C as held by me only if it is still free'.   2. You need an atomic check-and-take: a transaction.
  3. Eventual consistency and caches can show stale values, so they can't be the source of truth.
  4. A relational database with a conditional update and a unique constraint guarantees one winner.

Decision 5The payment webhook can arrive twice, and a fan may tap 'Pay' twice. How do you avoid processing a booking twice?

Why D is the answer

Service Bus duplicate detection + idempotent processing

What it is. Duplicate detection makes a Service Bus queue remember the MessageId of every message it accepted during a time window (up to 7 days), and silently drop a second message with the same ID. It must be switched on when the queue is created. Idempotent processing means handling the same message twice has the same effect as handling it once.

Why it fits here. Using the booking ID as the MessageId means a repeated webhook or a double tap produces the same ID, and Service Bus drops the copy. The worker also checks 'is this booking already confirmed?' before acting, which protects you outside the window or after a crash and redelivery.

How to think it through

  1. Duplicates come from the gateway's webhook and from fans tapping twice.
  2. Pick a natural unique key: the booking ID.
  3. Let the broker drop repeats (duplicate detection).
  4. Make the worker idempotent as a second layer.

Trigger words in the brief: "same webhook can arrive more than once""tap 'Pay' twice"

The architect's habit: Assume every message can arrive twice. Detect duplicates in the broker and make the handler idempotent.

Decision 6Fans constantly refresh the seat map during the sale. How do you keep it fast without hammering SQL?

Why A is the answer

Azure Managed Redis (cache)

What it is. Azure Managed Redis is a fully managed in-memory data store based on Redis. Reads come from memory in well under a millisecond. It's used as a cache in front of a slower system of record, with keys that expire after a set time.

Why it fits here. Seat-map reads are huge and repetitive during a sale. Serving them from Redis keeps SQL free for the writes that matter. A few seconds of staleness is safe because the seat is finally checked in the SQL transaction.

  1. During a sale, reads vastly outnumber writes.
  2. Every read hitting SQL helped crash the old system.
  3. A slightly stale map is fine because SQL makes the final decision.
  4. Cache in Redis with a short expiry; never make the cache the source of truth.

Decision 4How do you move 80 TB of drawings before the deadline over a 200 Mbps shared link?

The phrases to think about are now highlighted in the brief.

Why C is the answer

Azure Data Box (+ Azure File Sync to catch up)

What it is. Data Box is a rugged storage device Microsoft ships to you. You copy data onto it locally at network speed inside your building, ship it back, and Microsoft uploads it into your storage account or Azure file share. Azure File Sync then keeps a Windows file server and an Azure file share in sync.

Why it fits here. Copying 80 TB over a shared 200 Mbps link would take well over a month and slow the whole office down. Data Box moves the bulk in one to two weeks, including shipping. Changes made while the device was travelling are then synchronised by File Sync, so nothing is lost.

How to think it through

  1. 80 TB × 8 = 640 terabits. At 200 Mbps that's 3.2 million seconds, about 37 days at 100% use.
  2. The link is shared, so realistically it would take months.
  3. If the network can't do it in time, ship a device.
  4. Use online sync for the small changes afterwards.

The architect's habit: Do the arithmetic: data size divided by real bandwidth. If the answer is in weeks, ship a device.

Decision 7How do you meet the ERP target of RTO 4 hours and RPO 15 minutes if Central India has a regional outage?

Why B is the answer

Managed Instance failover group + Azure Site Recovery

What it is. A failover group keeps a readable geo-replica of a Managed Instance in another region and gives you listener names that always point to the current primary. Azure Site Recovery continuously replicates VMs to another region and fails them over in order, using recovery plans you can test without disrupting production.

Why it fits here. The database copy in South India is seconds behind, well inside the 15-minute RPO, and the app keeps using the same listener name after failover. Site Recovery replicates the ERP app VMs, and the recovery plan brings them up after the database, within the 4-hour RTO. Quarterly test failovers prove it works.

How to think it through

  1. An RPO of 15 minutes rules out nightly backups as the DR method.
  2. A regional outage needs another region; zones aren't enough.
  3. Database: a failover group (geo-replication with stable names).
  4. App VMs: Site Recovery with a tested recovery plan.

Trigger words in the brief: "within 4 hours""at most 15 minutes of data""all three plants stop"

The architect's habit: RPO picks the replication method; RTO picks how warm the standby must be. A zone is not a region.

Decision 8The board wants backups that survive a compromised administrator account. What do you configure?

Hint. Read the board member's quote twice. If an attacker steals one admin account, what could they do to today's backups? Which features take that power away from any single account?

The phrases to think about are now highlighted in the brief.

Why A is the answer

Immutable vault + multi-user authorisation (Resource Guard)

What it is. An immutable Recovery Services vault blocks any action that would delete recovery points before their retention ends; once locked, immutability can't be turned off. Soft delete keeps deleted backups recoverable. Multi-user authorisation (MUA) protects critical vault operations with a Resource Guard owned by a different team, whose permission is needed first.

Why it fits here. Even with a stolen backup admin account, an attacker can't shorten retention, delete recovery points or turn off protection, because those actions need the security team's Resource Guard permission in a separate subscription. This is the protection the peer company was missing.

How to think it through

  1. Assume one admin account is compromised.
  2. Backups controlled by the same hands as production can be destroyed first.
  3. Make recovery points undeletable (locked immutability, always-on soft delete).
  4. Split control: critical changes need a second team (MUA with a Resource Guard).

Trigger words in the brief: "even a compromised administrator account""ransomware""deleted its backups first"

The architect's habit: Ransomware design asks one question: can one stolen account destroy the backups? Make the answer no.

Mindset takeaway

A migration case is really four decisions under one deadline: what moves how (strategy), how the data travels (arithmetic), what stays (hybrid), and how it survives (DR and backup). Write each one down as a decision record.

After the cases

The habits these cases train

  • Underline the hard rules first. 'Never', 'must', 'provably', 'within 4 hours'. They decide the expensive parts.
  • Find the soft rules too. 'A few minutes later is fine' and 'next day is fine' are permissions to go asynchronous or cheaper.
  • Treat skills and deadlines as constraints. 'No Kubernetes' and 'lease ends in 10 months' rule out good technology for good reasons.
  • Do the arithmetic. Users per minute, terabytes over megabits, RPO in minutes. Numbers end arguments.
  • Separate the system of record from the speed layer. Caches, queues and CDNs make things fast; databases and vaults keep them true.
  • Ask 'what if one account is stolen?' Then design identity, guardrails and backups so the answer is boring.
  • Write down what you didn't use and why. It's the part of a design review that earns trust.
