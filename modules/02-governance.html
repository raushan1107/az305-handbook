02.0

The mental map

Everything in Azure governance hangs off one hierarchy. If you can draw this picture from memory, you can answer most governance questions in the exam. Settings applied at a higher level flow down to everything below.

Two words before the diagram

A tenant is your organisation's dedicated instance of Microsoft Entra ID (formerly Azure Active Directory) — your company's account with Microsoft. Every subscription, user and directory object you create lives inside exactly one tenant. A subscription is a billing and resource container inside that tenant. Every other layer in the hierarchy below is inside a subscription or sits above it to group subscriptions together.

Imagine this

Think of a large university. The university (tenant root) sets rules for everyone: no smoking, exams are proctored. Faculties (management groups) add their own: the chemistry faculty requires lab coats. Each department (subscription) gets its own budget. Inside a department, each lab (resource group) holds the equipment for one project. The equipment (resources) is what people actually use. A rule made at the university level reaches every test tube without anyone repeating it.

02.1

Why governance comes first

Definition

Cloud governance is the set of structures, rules and controls an organisation uses to make sure its cloud resources stay compliant, secure, cost-controlled and accountable as they grow.

In plain words

Governance answers four boring but critical questions for every resource: Who owns it? Who pays for it? Who can touch it? What rules must it follow? If you can't answer those, you don't have a cloud, you have a mess with a monthly invoice.

I often compare good governance to the guardrails on a mountain highway. Guardrails don't slow you down. In fact, they're the reason you dare to drive fast. Bad governance is a toll booth every 200 metres: so annoying that people find a dirt road around it. As an architect, you're designing guardrails, not toll booths.

Developer view

"Governance is the thing that blocks my deployment at 6 p.m. on a Friday."

Architect view

"Governance is how 40 teams can deploy on their own without a central team approving every change, because the rules are built into the platform."

Mindset takeaway

Governance is a one-way door (Module 01). Restructuring a hierarchy with 300 subscriptions and live workloads is painful. Spend the time up front.

02.2

Management groups

Containers for subscriptions

Definition

A management group is a container that organises subscriptions (and other management groups) into a hierarchy, so that Azure Policy and role assignments can be applied once and inherited by everything beneath it.

WhatFolders for subscriptions. Every tenant has one tenant root group at the top.
WhyApply rules and access to many subscriptions at once, consistently.
WhenAs soon as you have more than a handful of subscriptions, or know you will.

Facts worth remembering

  • Up to 10,000 management groups in a single directory.
  • Up to six levels of depth, not counting the root level or the subscription level.
  • Each management group and subscription has exactly one parent. No shared children.
  • The tenant root group can't be moved or deleted. By default nobody has access to it; a Global Administrator can elevate their own access to manage it.
  • New subscriptions land in the root group by default, unless you change the default management group, which you should.

How to design the hierarchy

The single most common mistake I see: copying the org chart into management groups. Org charts change every reorganisation; your hierarchy shouldn't have to.

Tempting but fragile
  • One MG per department head
  • Separate trees for Dev, Test and Prod at the top
  • Six levels deep "for flexibility"
Architect's approach
  • Group by what rules and access they need (e.g. internet-facing vs internal, regulated vs not)
  • Keep it shallow: three or four levels is plenty
  • Handle environments at the subscription level, not the MG level
Exam cue

"Apply a policy to all current and future subscriptions" or "to subscriptions across several departments" → assign at a management group. If a single subscription is mentioned, the subscription scope is usually enough.

Portal demoBuild a mini management group hierarchy~4 min

Goal: Show the tree from the mental map being created for real, and where inheritance comes from.

  1. Search Management groups. You'll see the Tenant Root Group. In a brand-new tenant you may first need to click Start using management groups.
  2. + Create › ID mg-az305-meridian, display name Meridian (Demo) › Submit.
  3. Open Meridian (Demo) and create two children the same way: Platform (Demo) and Landing zones (Demo).
  4. Back in the tree, find your demo subscription, open its … menu, choose Move and pick Landing zones (Demo).
  5. Open a management group and show its left menu: Policies and Access control (IAM) exist here too. Rules and roles can be assigned at this level.
  6. Open the Tenant Root Group › Settings: show the default management group for new subscriptions and the option to require permissions before people can create management groups.

Point out while you are there

  • The management group ID can't be changed later, only the display name. Name it properly.
  • Moving a subscription instantly changes the policies and roles it inherits. That's why moves are change-controlled in real companies.

Say: “One assignment at this level reaches every subscription below it, including ones that don't exist yet.”

Clean up: Move the subscription back, then delete the child groups before the parent.

02.3

Subscriptions

The unit of billing, scale and isolation

Definition

An Azure subscription is a logical container that is linked to a billing account and an Entra ID tenant. It is a boundary for billing, for service quotas and limits, and for access and policy management.

In plain words

A subscription is a bank account for cloud spending. Each one gets its own bill, its own limits (how many cores, how many public IPs…) and its own set of people with keys.

A subscription gives you three kinds of boundary. An architect uses all three when deciding "do we need another subscription?":

Billing boundaryCost shows up per subscription naturally. Easiest way to charge a business unit back.
Scale boundaryQuotas (vCPUs per region, number of resource groups and so on) are per subscription. Big workloads can hit them.
Security boundaryOwners of one subscription have no rights in another by default. Great for isolating production from experiments.

When to create a new subscription

  • A workload has a different compliance need (e.g. PCI card processing) and should be isolated.
  • A business unit needs a separate bill or budget.
  • You need to separate production from non-production so a dev mistake can't touch prod.
  • A workload is large enough to hit subscription limits.

Mature organisations treat subscriptions as cheap and disposable, and hand them out through an automated subscription vending process: a team asks, a pipeline creates the subscription in the right management group with the right policies, network and budget already applied.

Common trap

"One giant subscription for everything" feels simple in month one. By month twelve it has 3,000 resources, 90 people with Contributor, and a bill no one can explain. Subscriptions are free; confusion isn't.

Portal demoTour a subscription's three boundaries~2 min

Goal: Map billing, scale and security boundaries to real blades.

  1. Search Subscriptions and open your demo subscription.
  2. Billing boundary: Cost Management › Cost analysis: this month's cost, grouped by service.
  3. Scale boundary: Settings › Usage + quotas: filter by a region and show vCPU limits. These quotas are per subscription.
  4. Security boundary: Access control (IAM) › Role assignments: who holds the keys to this “bank account”.
  5. Back on Subscriptions, show + Add: creating a subscription needs a billing account or an agreement. Don't complete it; just show where vending starts.

Say: “Same three questions every time: who pays, how big can it get, and who has the keys.”

02.4

Resource groups

Things that live and die together

Definition

A resource group is a container that holds related resources for an Azure solution. Every resource belongs to exactly one resource group, and resource groups cannot be nested.

Imagine this

A resource group is a tiffin box. Everything for one meal goes in one box. You carry it together, you wash it together, and when lunch is over, you empty the whole box. You don't keep tomorrow's lunch in today's box.

The golden rule: group by lifecycle

Put resources in the same resource group when they are deployed, updated and deleted together. Deleting a resource group deletes everything inside it, so this is both a convenience and a danger.

Good grouping

rg-checkout-prod: the checkout web app, its database and its Key Vault. They ship together.

rg-network-hub: shared network that lives for years, owned by another team.

Poor grouping

rg-all-databases: databases from five apps with five lifecycles and five owners. One "cleanup" could wipe out all five.

Facts worth remembering

  • A resource group has a location, but that is only where its metadata is stored. Resources inside it can be in other regions.
  • Many resource types can be moved between resource groups and subscriptions; not all can, so check before you promise.
  • Resource groups are a natural scope for RBAC: "the checkout team is Contributor on rg-checkout-prod".
Portal demoCreate a resource group the architect's way~3 min

Goal: Create the demo resource group with tags from day one, and prove that the group's region is only metadata.

  1. Search Resource groups › + Create. Subscription: demo. Name: rg-az305-m02-demo. Region: Central India.
  2. Open the Tags tab and add Owner = your email, CostCenter = CC-DEMO, Environment = demo. Then Review + create › Create.
  3. Inside the new group: + Create › Storage account. Name staz305m02 plus your initials, region South India (a different region from the group), redundancy LRS › Review + create › Create.
  4. Open the resource group: a South India resource is sitting happily in a Central India group.

Point out while you are there

  • The group's region is only where its metadata lives.
  • Deleting the group deletes everything in it. That's the tiffin-box rule: group by lifecycle.

Say: “Tags on day one cost nothing. Tags added after a year of growth cost a project.”

02.5

Tags and cost visibility

Labels that make the bill readable

Definition

Tags are name/value pairs applied to resources, resource groups and subscriptions to add business metadata such as cost centre, owner or environment.

In plain words

The hierarchy can only organise things one way. Tags let you slice the same resources many ways: by owner, by cost centre, by data sensitivity, by project. They're the price stickers and name labels of the cloud.

A starter tag taxonomy

Tag nameExample valueAnswers the question
CostCenterCC-4021Who pays?
BusinessUnitOnlineWhich part of the company?
Ownercheckout-team@…Who do we call when it breaks or costs too much?
Environmentprod / dev / testHow careful must we be?
ApplicationcheckoutWhich workload does it belong to?
DataClassificationconfidentialHow sensitive is what it holds?

Facts worth remembering

  • Up to 50 tag pairs per resource, resource group or subscription.
  • Tags are not inherited by default. A tag on a resource group does not automatically appear on its resources.
  • To enforce or inherit tags, use Azure Policy (e.g. "Require a tag on resource groups" with Deny, and "Inherit a tag from the resource group if missing" with Modify).
  • Cost Management can also apply tag inheritance to cost data, and lets you set budgets with alerts per subscription or resource group.
  • Tags are plain text, readable by anyone with read access. Never put secrets in tags.
Exam cue

"Generate a cost report by department / cost centre" across resources in several resource groups → tags plus Cost Management. "Ensure every new resource has a CostCenter tag" → Azure Policy. The tag is the label, Policy is how you make sure it's there.

Portal demoProve tags don't inherit, then use them for cost~3 min

Goal: Show the inheritance gap, and how tags and budgets turn into cost visibility.

  1. Open the storage account › Tags. It has no tags, even though its resource group has three. Tags are not inherited.
  2. Add CostCenter = CC-DEMO manually › Apply. Ask: “Will anyone remember to do this for 3,000 resources?” Policy will fix it in the next demo.
  3. Subscription › Cost Management › Cost analysis › Group by › Tag › CostCenter. New resources can take up to a day to show cost, so use existing data if you have some.
  4. Cost Management › Budgets › + Add: scope the resource group, monthly, a small amount (for example ₹1,000), and an alert at 80% to your email › Create.

Point out while you are there

  • Tags are plain text that anyone with read access can see. Never put secrets in tags.
  • Budgets alert; they don't stop spending. Spending limits are an architecture and governance conversation, not a checkbox.

Say: “Labels are free. Untagged spend is a monthly mystery.”

02.6

Azure Policy

Rules that the platform enforces for you

Definition

Azure Policy evaluates Azure resources against business rules, defined in JSON, and can audit, block, modify or automatically deploy configurations so that resources stay compliant with organisational standards.

In plain words

Policy is a rulebook that Azure itself reads before saying "yes" to a deployment, and keeps re-reading afterwards. "Only in India regions." "No public IPs on databases." "Every resource group must have an Owner tag." You write the rule once; the platform does the policing forever.

The building blocks

Policy definitionOne rule: a condition ("if storage account allows public access") and an effect ("then deny").
InitiativeA named bundle of definitions with a shared goal, e.g. "PCI DSS v4" or "Microsoft cloud security benchmark".
AssignmentAttaching a definition or initiative to a scope: management group, subscription, resource group or resource.
Exclusion / exemptionCarving out a child scope from an assignment, or a time-bound, documented waiver for a specific resource.
Remediation taskFixes existing non-compliant resources for Modify and DeployIfNotExists effects, using a managed identity (an identity Azure creates and manages for a resource; Module 03).
Compliance viewA dashboard of what is and isn't compliant, at every scope, refreshed automatically.

Policy effects: what happens when a rule matches

EffectWhat it doesUse it when…
DenyBlocks the create or update request.The rule is non-negotiable: allowed regions, banned SKUs, no public endpoints.
AuditAllows it, but marks the resource non-compliant.You want visibility first, or you're testing a rule before switching to Deny.
AuditIfNotExistsFlags a resource when a related resource is missing."VMs without the monitoring agent extension."
DeployIfNotExistsDeploys the missing related resource automatically."Every new SQL server gets diagnostic settings sending logs to Log Analytics."
ModifyAdds, updates or removes tags or properties during create/update.Inheriting tags, enforcing a property without blocking anyone.
AppendAdds fields to the request (older effect, Modify is usually preferred).Adding a specific IP rule to storage firewall settings.
DenyActionBlocks a specific action, such as delete, on matching resources.Protecting critical resources from deletion at scale.
DisabledSwitches the rule off without removing the assignment.Temporarily pausing a rule, or parameterising it per scope.

Where Policy sits in a deployment

Every create, update or delete in Azure goes through Azure Resource Manager (ARM). That's what makes Policy so powerful: it doesn't matter if you used the portal, CLI, Bicep, Terraform or a pipeline. Everyone passes the same checkpoint.

Common trap

Assigning a new Deny policy does not delete or fix resources that already break the rule. They simply show up as non-compliant. To fix existing resources, you need Modify or DeployIfNotExists with a remediation task, or a manual clean-up.

Good practice: roll out like a feature

Start a new rule as Audit at a broad scope, look at the compliance report, talk to the affected teams, then switch to Deny. Policy-as-code in a Git repo, deployed through a pipeline, makes every rule reviewable and reversible.

What about Azure Blueprints?

Older material uses Azure Blueprints to package role assignments, policies and templates together. Blueprints is being retired in phases: no new blueprint definitions since 31 July 2026, and full retirement on 31 January 2027, when anything not exported is deleted. The modern replacement is Template Specs and Deployment Stacks for the infrastructure, plus Azure Policy for the rules. If an exam question offers both, prefer the modern combination unless the wording clearly points to Blueprints.

Portal demoAssign “Allowed locations” and watch it block a deployment~6 min

Goal: Let learners see Policy refuse a deployment from an Owner, which makes “Policy is about the resource, not the person” land.

Before you start: Do steps 1–4 at the start of the module or before a break. A new assignment can take several minutes, sometimes up to about 30, to start enforcing.

  1. Search Policy › Authoring › Definitions › search Allowed locations and open it. Show the JSON briefly: if location is not in the list, then deny.
  2. Assign policy › Basics: Scope = demo subscription › rg-az305-m02-demo. Assignment name: Demo – India only.
  3. Parameters: untick Only show parameters that need input if the list is hidden, then choose Central India and South India.
  4. Non-compliance messages: “Meridian: only India regions are allowed.” › Review + create › Create.
  5. Later, in the resource group: + Create › Storage account, region East US › Review + create. Validation fails, the request is disallowed by policy, and your custom message appears.
  6. Policy › Compliance: find your assignment and show compliant and non-compliant resources (the first scan can lag).

Point out while you are there

  • You are Owner, and you were still blocked. RBAC said yes; Policy said no.
  • Existing resources aren't deleted by a Deny policy. They're only reported as non-compliant.

Say: “Write the rule once, and the platform enforces it for every portal click, CLI command and pipeline, forever.”

Clean up: Policy › Assignments › delete Demo – India only.

02.7

Azure Policy vs RBAC

This is one of the most-tested distinctions in the whole exam, and the one people confuse most. So let's slow down.

Imagine this

You walk into an office building. Your ID card decides which floors you can enter: that's RBAC. It's about you. The building's fire code says no room may hold more than 40 people and fire exits can never be blocked, no matter who you are, even the CEO. That's Policy. It's about the thing.

Azure RBACAzure Policy
FocusWho can perform which actionsWhat state resources must be in
Question"Can this user/app delete VMs in this RG?""Are VMs here allowed to be this size, in this region, without a tag?"
DefaultDeny: no access until a role is grantedAllow: everything permitted until a rule says otherwise
Applies toUsers, groups, service principals, managed identitiesResources and their properties, regardless of who deploys
ScopeMG, subscription, RG, resourceMG, subscription, RG, resource
Typical exam phrase"least privilege", "allow the team to manage…""ensure", "prevent", "enforce", "audit compliance"

We'll go much deeper into RBAC (roles, scopes, custom roles, Privileged Identity Management) in Module 03, because RBAC is fundamentally an identity decision.

Portal demoCheck access vs check compliance~2 min

Goal: Put the two gates side by side in the portal.

  1. Resource group › Access control (IAM) › Check access › View my access: you are Owner. Gate 1 (RBAC) says yes.
  2. Recall the blocked storage account from the previous demo. Gate 2 (Policy) said no.
  3. Roles tab › search Contributor › open it and view Permissions or JSON: Actions: * but NotActions include Microsoft.Authorization/*/Write. Contributor can build anything but can't grant access.

Say: “IAM answers ‘who may act’. Policy answers ‘what is allowed to exist’. You need both.”

02.8

Resource locks

Definition

A resource lock prevents users from accidentally deleting or modifying a resource, resource group or subscription, overriding any permissions they have. The two lock levels are CanNotDelete and ReadOnly.

Imagine this

A lock is the plastic cover over the big red emergency button. It doesn't stop the right person pressing it on purpose (they lift the cover first). It stops someone leaning on it by accident.

CanNotDelete

Users can read and modify, but not delete. Perfect for the production hub network, the central Log Analytics workspace or the Key Vault holding your certificates.

ReadOnly

Users can read but not modify or delete. Use sparingly: it can break things you don't expect, because some "read" operations are technically writes.

  • Locks are inherited: a lock on a resource group protects every resource in it.
  • Locks apply even to Owners. To delete, someone with permission to manage locks must remove the lock first, which is the point.
  • Locks protect the management plane (the resource itself), not the data inside. A lock on a storage account doesn't stop someone deleting blobs.
Common trap

A ReadOnly lock on a storage account blocks listing its access keys (that's a POST operation), so apps and portal views that rely on keys break. A ReadOnly lock on a resource group with an App Service can stop scaling and deployments. Prefer CanNotDelete unless you truly mean "frozen".

Exam cue

"Prevent accidental deletion" → CanNotDelete lock. "Prevent anyone from creating resources of type X" → Policy, not a lock. "Prevent a specific team from deleting" → RBAC (give them a role without delete rights).

Portal demoLock it, try to delete it~2 min

Goal: Show that a lock beats even Owner permissions, and that locks inherit.

  1. Resource group › Settings › Locks › + Add: name do-not-delete, lock type Delete › OK. (The portal says Delete and Read-only; the exam says CanNotDelete and ReadOnly.)
  2. Overview › Delete resource group › type the name › Delete. It fails because the group is locked.
  3. Open the storage account › Locks: the lock shows as inherited from the parent.
  4. Back on the resource group › Locks: delete the lock (you'll need it gone for clean-up).

Point out while you are there

  • Locks protect the resource (management plane), not the data. A delete lock on a storage account doesn't stop someone deleting blobs.

Say: “It's the plastic cover over the red button: it doesn't stop you pressing it on purpose, it stops you pressing it by accident.”

02.9

Azure landing zones

Everything above, packaged as a ready foundation

Definition

An Azure landing zone is a pre-configured, scalable environment, built from management groups, subscriptions, policies, identity, networking and monitoring, into which workloads can be deployed securely and consistently. It is part of the Cloud Adoption Framework's "Ready" phase.

Imagine this

A landing zone is a plot in a well-planned township. Before anyone builds a house, the developer has already laid the roads, water, electricity, drainage, a security gate and the society rules. A family (workload team) only has to build their house. Nobody has to dig their own well or run their own power line.

Two kinds of landing zone

Platform landing zones

Shared services owned by a central platform team: Identity (e.g. domain controllers), Management (central Log Analytics, automation) and Connectivity (hub network, firewall, ExpressRoute/VPN, DNS).

Application landing zones

Subscriptions handed to workload teams, pre-wired to the platform. Often split into Corp (internal, connected to on-premises) and Online (internet-facing).

How you get one

  • The Azure landing zone accelerator in the portal deploys the reference architecture through a guided wizard.
  • Infrastructure-as-code versions exist in Bicep and Terraform, for teams that want the foundation in Git with pipelines.
  • You can start small (a "start small and expand" approach) and grow into the full design. The hierarchy is the part you should get right on day zero; everything else can mature.
Exam cue

"Provide a repeatable, governed environment for new workloads", "enterprise-scale foundation", or "prepare the environment before migration" → Azure landing zones (CAF Ready phase).

Portal demoPeek inside the landing zone accelerator~2 min

Goal: Show that a landing zone is everything in this module (and the next two), packaged as a wizard.

  1. On Microsoft Learn, search “Azure landing zone portal accelerator” and click its Deploy to Azure button. The wizard opens in your portal.
  2. Walk the tabs without deploying: deployment location and management group prefix, management and monitoring (Log Analytics, Defender), network topology (hub and spoke or Virtual WAN), identity, and landing zone policies.
  3. Close the wizard. Nothing is created until the final tab.

Point out while you are there

  • Every tab maps to a module of this handbook: governance, monitoring, identity and networking.
  • Real teams usually deploy the same design with the Bicep or Terraform versions, so it lives in Git.

Say: “Roads, water and electricity before the first house. That's a landing zone.”

02.11

Recap and self-check

Architect's governance checklist

  • The management group hierarchy is shallow and organised by policy and access needs.
  • Subscriptions separate billing, environments and compliance boundaries.
  • Resource groups follow lifecycle: things that deploy and die together live together.
  • A tag taxonomy exists and is enforced by Policy, not by hope.
  • Non-negotiable rules use Deny; everything else starts as Audit.
  • Critical shared resources have CanNotDelete locks.
  • New workloads land in a landing zone, never in a hand-built subscription.

Self-check

1. You must ensure resources in all current and future subscriptions can only be deployed to two specific regions. Where and how?

Assign the built-in Allowed locations policy with a Deny effect at the management group that contains those subscriptions (or the top-level company management group, such as Meridian). New subscriptions placed under it inherit the rule automatically.

2. Finance wants a monthly cost report per cost centre. Resources for one cost centre are spread across several resource groups. What do you recommend?

A CostCenter tag, enforced and inherited with Azure Policy, then group by that tag in Cost Management. Resource groups alone can't express this because cost centres cut across them.

3. A user with the Owner role can't delete a resource group. Name two possible reasons.

A CanNotDelete (or ReadOnly) lock on the group or a parent, or a policy with the DenyAction effect blocking deletion. Owners aren't exempt from either.

4. You assigned a Deny policy "storage accounts must not allow public blob access". Will existing non-compliant accounts be fixed?

No. Deny only affects new creates and updates. Existing accounts show as non-compliant. To fix them, use a policy with Modify and a remediation task, or update them manually.

5. Why is "one management group per department" usually a poor design?

Departments change with reorganisations, and departments rarely need different rules. Management groups should reflect differences in policy and access; departments are better represented as subscriptions and tags.

Carry this into Module 03

Governance decides the rules of the building. Now we need to decide who gets in, how they prove who they are, and what their badge allows. That's identity.
