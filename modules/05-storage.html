05.1

Why storage is a design decision, not a default

Imagine this

Think about how a family in an Indian city stores things. Daily-use utensils sit on the kitchen counter (fast to reach, but counter space is expensive). Winter blankets go into the loft (cheaper space, a bit of effort to reach). Property papers go into a bank locker (very safe, very cheap per item, but you need an appointment to get them out). And the really important ones get a photocopy at your parents' house in another city, in case something happens to yours. That's the whole of Azure storage: hot tier, cool tier, archive tier and geo-redundancy.

Developer view

"I need somewhere to save uploaded images. I'll create a storage account with the defaults and paste the connection string into the config file."

Architect view

"How much data, growing how fast? How often is each object read after the first week? Must it survive a region outage, or is a zone enough? Is any of it under legal retention? Who should reach it, and over which network? No connection strings: managed identity and RBAC."

In every batch I teach, I ask one question: "What's the most expensive storage mistake?" People guess "using premium disks". The real answer is storing everything in the hot tier forever. Storage cost grows quietly, every month, for data nobody has opened since the day it was written.

Mindset takeaway

Every byte has a temperature (how often it's read), a lifespan (how long it must exist) and a blast radius (what failure it must survive). Design for all three and storage becomes cheap and boring, which is exactly what you want.

05.2

Storage accounts

The container for your storage services

Definition

An Azure storage account contains your Azure Storage data objects: blobs, files, queues and tables. It provides a unique namespace for that data, reachable from anywhere over HTTP or HTTPS, and it's where you set redundancy, networking, encryption and access options that apply to everything inside.

In plain words

A storage account is a building with one address. Inside it there are four kinds of rooms: Blob, Files, Queues and Tables. The building's rules (which city it's in, how many copies, who gets through the gate) apply to every room. So when two sets of data need different rules, they need different storage accounts.

Account types: pick the right building

TypeDisk underneathServicesRedundancyUse it for
Standard general-purpose v2HDD-basedBlob, Files, Queues, Tables, Data Lake Gen2All optionsThe default for almost everything
Premium block blobsSSDBlock and append blobsLRS, ZRSLow latency, high transaction rates (IoT, AI, analytics hot paths)
Premium file sharesSSDAzure Files (SMB and NFS)LRS, ZRSDatabases on shares, heavy enterprise file workloads
Premium page blobsSSDPage blobs onlyLRS, ZRSSpecial cases; managed disks cover most needs

Facts worth remembering

  • The name is 3–24 characters, lowercase letters and numbers only, and globally unique (it becomes part of a public DNS name).
  • About 250 storage accounts per region per subscription by default (a quota increase is possible). A single account holds petabytes, so you rarely run out.
  • Premium accounts support only LRS and ZRS: no geo-replication. That single fact answers a surprising number of exam questions.
  • Legacy account types (general-purpose v1, legacy Blob storage accounts) are being retired. Upgrade them to general-purpose v2; it's a one-click, in-place change.
Exam cue

"Lowest latency for blobs" or "high transaction rate, small objects" → Premium block blob account. "Need geo-redundancy" + "premium" in the same question → you can't have both on one account; the answer usually changes to Standard GPv2 with GRS/GZRS. "Data with different compliance rules" → separate storage accounts.

05.3

Redundancy: how many copies, how far apart

Definition

Azure Storage always keeps multiple copies of your data. The redundancy option decides where those copies live: in one datacentre (LRS), across availability zones in the primary region (ZRS), or additionally in a secondary region hundreds of kilometres away (GRS and GZRS). Read-access variants (RA-GRS, RA-GZRS) let you read from the secondary region at any time.

In plain words

Every option keeps at least three copies. The only question is what disaster those copies can survive: a broken disk, a burnt-down building, or a whole city going offline.

Imagine this

LRS is three photocopies of your property papers in the same cupboard. ZRS is one copy at home, one at your office and one at your brother's flat, all in Bengaluru. GRS adds a set at your parents' house in Chennai, posted to them a little after each change. RA-GRS means your parents can read that copy any time, not only after a disaster.

The decision in one table

Requirement in the questionChoose
Cheapest; data can be recreated; or the law says data must stay in one placeLRS
Stay available during a zone (datacentre) outage, with synchronous copiesZRS
Survive a regional disaster; secondary is for recovery onlyGRS (or GZRS for zone protection too)
Applications must be able to read during a regional outageRA-GRS or RA-GZRS
Maximum durability and availability for critical dataRA-GZRS

Facts worth remembering

  • The secondary region is the region's pair: Central India ↔ South India, Germany West Central ↔ Germany North. You don't pick it.
  • With GRS (no "RA"), the secondary is not readable until a failover happens.
  • Customer-managed failover lets you promote the secondary yourself. After an unplanned failover, the account becomes LRS in the new region until you re-enable geo-replication.
  • The Last Sync Time property tells you how far behind the secondary is. Use it to estimate data loss before you fail over.
Common trap

Redundancy is not backup. If someone deletes a blob or ransomware encrypts it, that change is faithfully replicated to every copy within minutes. Protection against mistakes comes from soft delete, versioning, immutability and backup, which we cover in 05.5.

Exam cue

"Read data even if the primary region is unavailable" → RA-GRS / RA-GZRS. "Survive a datacentre / zone failure, no data loss" → ZRS (synchronous). "Survive a regional outage" → GRS/GZRS. "Minimise cost, data can be regenerated" → LRS.

Say: “Every option keeps three copies. Redundancy is only about which disaster those copies can survive.”

05.4

Blob storage, tiers and lifecycle

Object storage for anything you can name with a URL

Definition

Azure Blob Storage is Microsoft's object storage for massive amounts of unstructured data, such as text, images, video, backups and logs. Objects (blobs) are stored in containers and accessed over HTTP/HTTPS through REST APIs, SDKs, the Azure CLI or tools such as AzCopy and Storage Explorer.

In plain words

Blob is a giant warehouse of labelled boxes. You don't open a box and edit one line inside it; you put a whole box in, or take a whole box out, using its label (the URL). That's why it's perfect for photos, videos, PDFs and backups, and wrong for a database file someone edits all day.

Block blobThe normal one: files, images, video, backups. Up to about 190 TiB per blob (TiB, the tebibyte, is the binary unit Azure uses for storage limits: 1 TiB is about 1.1 TB). 99% of what you store.
Append blobYou can only add to the end. Built for logs and audit trails.
Page blobRandom read/write in 512-byte pages. What disks were built on; today you use managed disks instead.

Access tiers: pay for storing, or pay for reading

To give you a feel for the numbers (illustrative, LRS, Central India): 1 TB in Hot costs roughly ₹1,500–1,700 a month, in Cool about half that, in Cold roughly a fifth, and in Archive under ₹200. For 200 TB of old file-share archives, that difference pays a salary.

Facts worth remembering

  • The account has a default tier (Hot or Cool, plus Cold on newer accounts); each blob can override it.
  • Archive is offline. To read a blob you rehydrate it to an online tier: standard priority can take up to 15 hours; high priority is often under an hour for smaller blobs, at a higher price.
  • Archive isn't available on ZRS, GZRS or RA-GZRS accounts. Use LRS, GRS or RA-GRS for archive data.
  • Lifecycle management policies move or delete blobs based on age (since creation, last modification or last access) and on prefixes or blob index tags. They run about once a day.
Developer view

"Everything goes in Hot because Hot is fast, and I don't want to think about it."

Architect view

"Hot for the first 30 days while customers actually view it, then a lifecycle rule. I'll enable last-access tracking so popular old images stay hot, and I'll put a delete rule on the end so we don't pay for data the law no longer requires."

Exam cue

"Rarely accessed, retrieval within hours is acceptable, minimise storage cost" → Archive. "Accessed rarely but must be available immediately" → Cool or Cold. "Automatically move data to cheaper tiers as it ages" → lifecycle management policy. "Serve images to users worldwide with low latency" → Blob + Azure Front Door (CDN caching).

Name check

The classic "Azure CDN" profiles are being retired. For new designs, content delivery for blobs is done with Azure Front Door (Standard or Premium), which includes CDN caching. Module 11 covers it.

05.5

Protecting blobs from mistakes and attacks

Redundancy protects you from Azure's failures. This section protects you from your own: the accidental delete, the overwritten file, the script that ran against production, and the ransomware that encrypted everything it could reach.

Soft delete (blobs and containers)Deleted items are kept for 1–365 days and can be restored. Turn it on for every production account; 7–14 days is a common default.
VersioningEvery overwrite keeps the previous version automatically. Protects against "someone uploaded the wrong file over the right one".
Point-in-time restoreRoll a set of block blobs back to how they looked at an earlier time. Needs versioning, change feed and soft delete.
Immutable storage (WORM)Time-based retention ("nobody can change or delete this for 7 years") or legal hold ("until the case is closed"). Once a policy is locked, not even the subscription Owner, or Microsoft, can shorten it.
Resource lockA CanNotDelete lock (Module 02) stops the account being deleted. It does not protect the data inside.
Azure Backup for blobsOperational backup (local, continuous) and vaulted backup (copies to a separate Backup vault), covered in Module 08.
Imagine this

Soft delete is the recycle bin. Versioning is Google Docs' version history. Immutable storage is the registrar's office: once a sale deed is registered, nobody, not even the person who registered it, can tear out that page.

Common trap

Locking an immutability policy is irreversible. You can't delete the container, or the account, until every blob has passed its retention period. In demos and tests, always leave the policy unlocked.

Exam cue

"Data must be non-erasable and non-modifiable", "WORM", "SEC 17a-4 (a US financial-records rule) / regulatory retention" → immutable storage with time-based retention. "Keep data until litigation ends" → legal hold. "Recover from accidental deletion" → soft delete. "Recover a previous version of an overwritten file" → versioning.

Portal demoDelete a blob, bring it back, and see a WORM policy~3 min

Goal: Show soft delete and versioning rescuing a mistake, and show what immutability looks like.

  1. In images, upload logo.png again with Overwrite ticked. Open the blob › Versions: the previous version is still there.
  2. Back in the container, select the blob › Delete. Turn on Show deleted blobs: it's still listed as deleted › select it › Undelete.
  3. Container › Settings › Access policy › under Immutable blob storage + Add policy › Time-based retention, 1 day › Save. Leave it unlocked.
  4. Try to delete the blob: refused. Show the Lock policy option and explain that locking is irreversible. Then delete the unlocked policy.

Point out while you are there

  • Redundancy would have replicated the delete to every copy. Soft delete is what saved it.

Say: “The recycle bin, the version history and the registrar's office: three different ways of saying 'you can't lose this'.”

Clean up: Delete the immutability policy before you finish (it must be unlocked).

05.6

Azure Files and Azure File Sync

The shared drive, without the file server

Definition

Azure Files offers fully managed file shares in the cloud, accessible through the industry-standard SMB and NFS protocols and the Azure Files REST API. Shares can be mounted concurrently by cloud and on-premises clients. Azure File Sync caches Azure file shares on Windows Servers on-premises or in the cloud, keeping the share in Azure as the source of truth.

In plain words

Azure Files is the S: drive your office has always had, except Microsoft runs the server, patches it, and never runs out of disk. Apps that expect a normal folder path work without code changes. That's why it's the natural answer for "lift and shift a file server" or "an old app that writes to a network share".

Tiers and protocols

OptionMediaProtocolsGood for
Premium (FileStorage account)SSD, provisionedSMB and NFS 4.1Databases, latency-sensitive apps, Linux NFS workloads
Transaction optimisedHDD (Standard)SMBHeavy-transaction workloads that don't need SSD latency
HotHDD (Standard)SMBGeneral team shares
CoolHDD (Standard)SMBOnline archive shares, File Sync cloud endpoints

Newer Standard accounts can also use the provisioned (v2) billing model, where you choose capacity, IOPS and throughput up front instead of paying per transaction. Check what your region offers.

Facts worth remembering

  • A share can grow to 100 TiB. Up to 200 share snapshots per share give users "Previous Versions" in Windows Explorer.
  • SMB uses port 445, which many ISPs and company firewalls block. From on-premises, use VPN or ExpressRoute with a private endpoint, or File Sync (which talks over HTTPS 443).
  • NFS shares are Premium only and are reached over a private network, not the public internet.
  • Identity-based access keeps your existing NTFS ACLs, so permissions migrate along with the files.
Developer view

"We'll rewrite the app to use Blob storage instead of the file share. It's more cloud-native."

Architect view

"Rewrite costs months. Azure Files lets us migrate now with zero code change. If Blob is better long term, that's a later modernisation step with its own business case."

Exam cue

"Replace on-premises file servers", "SMB share", "lift and shift an app that uses a file share" → Azure Files. "Keep a local cache at branch offices", "centralise file servers but keep performance on-prem", "cloud tiering" → Azure File Sync. "NFS for Linux" → Premium Azure Files (or Azure NetApp Files for the most demanding cases).

05.7

Managed disks

The hard drives of your virtual machines

Definition

Azure managed disks are block-level storage volumes managed by Azure and used with Azure Virtual Machines. You choose the disk type and size (and, for some types, performance), and Azure handles placement, replication and availability. Each VM has an OS disk, optional data disks and, depending on the size, a temporary disk.

In plain words

A managed disk is the hard drive of a VM. You don't worry about which storage account it lives in (that's what "managed" means). Your only real decision is how fast it must be, and fast disks cost more, so you match the disk to the workload.

Disk typeMax IOPS (per disk)Max throughputOS disk?Use for
Ultra Diskup to 400,000up to 10,000 MB/sNoSAP HANA, top-tier databases, sub-millisecond latency
Premium SSD v2up to 80,000up to 1,200 MB/sNoProduction SQL Server, Oracle, big data: set IOPS and throughput independently of size
Premium SSDup to 20,000up to 900 MB/sYesProduction VMs, OS disks of important servers
Standard SSDup to 6,000up to 750 MB/sYesWeb servers, light apps, dev/test
Standard HDDup to 2,000up to 500 MB/sYesBackups, non-critical, infrequent access

Figures are per-disk maximums at the largest sizes and change over time. A VM size also has its own IOPS and throughput cap, so a fast disk on a small VM is throttled by the VM.

Imagine this

Disk types are like roads. Standard HDD is a village road: fine for a bullock cart. Standard SSD is a city road. Premium SSD is a four-lane highway. Ultra Disk is a dedicated expressway with no traffic signals. But your car (the VM size) still has a top speed, and a Nano on the expressway is still a Nano.

Facts worth remembering

  • Managed disks are LRS by default. ZRS disks (Premium SSD and Standard SSD) keep the disk available through a zone outage.
  • Shared disks let several VMs attach the same disk, for clustered apps such as Windows Server Failover Clustering.
  • Ephemeral OS disks live on the VM's local cache: free, fast, and lost when the VM is reimaged. Perfect for stateless scale sets.
  • Snapshots are incremental and cheap; use them before risky changes. Real backup is Azure Backup (Module 08).
  • Encryption at rest (server-side encryption) is always on. Add customer-managed keys through a disk encryption set, and encryption at host to cover temp disks and caches too.
Name check

Azure Disk Encryption (BitLocker or dm-crypt inside the guest) is on its way out: Microsoft has announced its retirement and recommends encryption at host for new designs. Older practice questions may still show ADE as the answer for "encrypt the OS disk".

Exam cue

"Sub-millisecond latency", "SAP HANA", "highest IOPS" → Ultra Disk. "Tune IOPS and throughput independently of capacity" → Premium SSD v2. "Production workload with an SLA, OS disk" → Premium SSD. "Dev/test, lowest cost" → Standard SSD or HDD. "Disk must survive a zone failure" → ZRS managed disk.

05.8

Storage security

Three gates every request passes through

A storage account has a public DNS name by default. That makes security a design topic, not a checkbox. Think of every request passing three gates in order.

Gate 2 in detail: the ways to authorise a request

MethodWhat it isArchitect's verdict
Microsoft Entra ID + RBACUsers, groups or managed identities get data roles such as Storage Blob Data ReaderThe default. No secrets, full audit, works with Conditional Access.
User delegation SASA time-limited URL signed with Entra credentials (max 7 days)Best way to give a partner temporary access to a specific blob or container.
Service SASTime-limited URL for one service, signed with the account keyAcceptable; pair with a stored access policy so you can revoke it.
Account SASTime-limited URL for one or more services, signed with the account keyWide reach; the only "revoke" is rotating the key.
Shared keyThe account access key: full control of everythingTreat it as root. Disable shared key access where you can.
Imagine this

The account key is the master key to the whole housing society: whoever has it opens every flat. A SAS is a visitor's pass from the security guard: it says which flat, which hours, and expires tonight. Entra ID with RBAC is the resident's ID card, linked to a real person, checked every time, and cancelled the day they move out.

Common trap

Being Owner of a storage account doesn't give you a data role. Owner can read the account keys (and so can do everything), but if keys are disabled, an Owner without Storage Blob Data Reader can't list a single blob. It's the same control-plane vs data-plane split you saw with Key Vault in Module 03.

Gates 1 and 3: network and encryption

  • Private endpoints give the account a private IP in your VNet (one per sub-service: blob, file, dfs…). Combine with public network access: Disabled. Service endpoints are the older, simpler option that keeps the public endpoint but restricts it to chosen subnets.
  • Encryption at rest is AES-256, always on, and can't be turned off. Choose Microsoft-managed keys (default) or customer-managed keys in Key Vault or Managed HSM, accessed by the account's managed identity.
  • Infrastructure encryption adds a second layer of encryption. It can only be enabled when the account is created.
  • Microsoft Defender for Storage detects unusual access and can scan uploaded blobs for malware, which is valuable on any container that accepts uploads from outside.
Exam cue

"Grant temporary, limited access to an external partner" → SAS (preferably user delegation SAS). "Be able to revoke a SAS" → stored access policy. "App in Azure accesses storage without storing credentials" → managed identity + RBAC. "Storage reachable only from the VNet, no public IP" → private endpoint. "Company must control and rotate the encryption keys" → customer-managed keys in Key Vault.

  3. Select logo.png › Generate SAS: signing method User delegation key, permission Read, expiry in 1 hour › Generate SAS token and URL. Open the URL in a private window: it works, until the expiry.
  4. Storage account › Settings › Configuration: show Allow storage account key access (set it to Disabled to force Entra ID; keys and account SAS stop working) and Minimum TLS version.
  5. Security + networking › Networking: set public network access to Disabled (or selected networks) and show the Private endpoint connections tab. Refresh the container from your laptop: blocked.

Point out while you are there

  • Security + networking › Encryption: switch to customer-managed keys points to a Key Vault, accessed through a managed identity, just like Module 03.
  • Microsoft Defender for Cloud on the account: Defender for Storage and malware scanning on upload.

Say: “Owner manages the building. Reading what's inside the flats needs a separate key, and that's exactly the point.”

Clean up: Set public network access back to enabled if you want to continue using the account; otherwise just delete the resource group.

05.11

Recap and self-check

Architect's storage checklist

  • Each data set mapped to a service by its access pattern: object, file share or block device.
  • Storage accounts split by real differences: region, redundancy, network, compliance.
  • Redundancy chosen by the disaster it must survive (disk, zone or region), and whether the secondary must be readable.
  • Access tiers and lifecycle policies set, including a final delete rule.
  • Soft delete and versioning on; immutability where regulations demand it.
  • Disk types matched to IOPS needs, and to the VM size's limits.
  • Entra ID + RBAC first, SAS only when needed, shared key disabled.
  • Private endpoints, public access off, and encryption keys owned where compliance requires it.

Self-check

1. A storage account must allow applications to read data even while the primary region is down. Which redundancy option?

RA-GRS or RA-GZRS. Plain GRS/GZRS keeps the secondary copy but doesn't let you read it until a failover.

2. Compliance documents are read about once a year, must be kept for 10 years, and a retrieval delay of several hours is fine. Cheapest design?

Blob storage in the Archive tier, moved there automatically by a lifecycle management policy, with a delete rule at 10 years. Add immutable storage if they must be tamper-proof.

3. A company wants to retire its branch-office file servers but keep fast local access to frequently used files. What do you recommend?

Azure Files as the central share plus Azure File Sync with cloud tiering on a small Windows Server in each branch.

4. A partner needs to upload files to one container for the next 48 hours, and you must be able to cut them off early. How?

Give a SAS limited to that container with write/create permissions and a 48-hour expiry. Use a user delegation SAS (signed with Entra credentials; revoke by revoking the user delegation key) or a service SAS tied to a stored access policy you can delete.

5. You need a Premium storage account and geo-redundancy. Can you have both?

No. Premium accounts support only LRS and ZRS. Either use Standard GPv2 with GRS/GZRS, or keep Premium and replicate at the application level (or with object replication to a second account).

6. A production SQL Server VM needs 40,000 IOPS on a 1 TiB data disk without paying for a much larger disk. Which disk type?

Premium SSD v2, which lets you set IOPS and throughput independently of capacity (and check the VM size can deliver 40,000 IOPS too).

Carry this into Module 06

Storage holds bytes. A database adds meaning to them: structure, queries, transactions and consistency. The same three questions follow you there: how fast must it be, how many copies, and who can touch it?
